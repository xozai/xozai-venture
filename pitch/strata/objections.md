# Phase 3 — Objections: Strata Civic Solutions

Scribe, 2026-09-01. Living document — update after every real investor or city meeting.
Rule: answer with the strongest true answer, or concede. A conceded objection costs one
meeting; a bluffed one costs the relationship.

## The 18 hardest questions

**1. "CivicPlus and Granicus already shipped AI. Why doesn't this get bundled away?"**
They shipped it for city *staff* — agenda drafting, minutes formatting, records
summarization. Our user has no staff and no seat in those systems. An incumbent would have to
build a different product for a different person and sell it into a body that did not procure
it. That is not an upsell, it is a new go-to-market. It is still the biggest structural risk
in the deck and it is on the risk slide.

**2. "Ordinal AI has $1M and seven cities. You have zero."**
True, and stated on slide 9. Ordinal sells across departments — planners, clerks, code
enforcement, 311, plus a public chatbot. Broad surface, shallow wedge. We sell one job to one
person: the official's hour before a vote. In a category this young the risk is not that
someone is ahead; it is that nobody is deep enough to be the default for a specific buyer.

**3. "Your SAM is $2.2M. How is this venture-scale?"**
At this ask it is not a venture bet, and we would rather say so now. $2.2M is the Texas
10k–50k tier counted one city at a time. The national frame is 19,519 municipalities and
16,360 towns; we have not modeled it and will not claim it. If a fund needs a $100M outcome
underwritten today, this is the wrong stage of this company.

**4. "You're asking for $40k. Why is a pitch deck involved at all?"**
Because the accepted model says the business reaches positive operating income in month 19
without a round, and the only thing in the way is a $29k cash trough at month 18. The honest
ask is the one the model supports. If an investor wants to fund acceleration instead, that is
a different conversation with a different number, and it needs a financed model run first.

**5. "Schertz is a demo city that doesn't pay. Is anything real?"**
The product is real, live, and in daily reach of a city that publishes its record. The
commercial relationship is not. We modeled it as zero paying cities from month one precisely
so no plan of ours depends on Schertz converting.

**6. "Hallucination. One wrong answer in a public meeting and you're done."**
Which is why the citation is the product, not a feature of it. Every answer resolves to a
page in a document the city itself published; the official verifies before they speak. The
tool assists, it does not draft the record or determine the vote — so an error is an
embarrassment and a churn event, not statutory liability. We chose that boundary deliberately.

**7. "What stops a city from doing this with ChatGPT and a folder of PDFs?"**
Nothing stops them trying, and some will. What they get is an answer without a verifiable
page, no ingestion of the city's full published record, and no one accountable when the
retrieval is wrong. Our competitor is not a model, it is a workflow — and the workflow we
have to beat is Ctrl+F, which is free.

**8. "One founder doing every demo. What's the real growth ceiling?"**
0.35 new paying cities per month in the base case, compounding 5% monthly — that is the
model's honest ceiling and the reason hiring timing is the single most sensitive input.
Delaying the first contractor three months takes the capital need from $29k to $11k; hiring
an engineer at month 16 instead of 28 sank an earlier draft to −$101k. Sequencing is the
whole discipline of this plan.

**9. "Your champion is term-limited. What happens at the next election?"**
Real and specific to this category. Two mitigations: the contract is with the city and lives
on the city manager's software line, not with an individual; and board and commission
volunteers — who outnumber elected officials about 5:1 and turn over on a different cycle —
are on the same contract. Renewal risk concentrates in the Aug–Sep budget window, which is
where the retention motion is aimed.

**10. "Why won't procurement bury you?"**
Because we priced to stay out of it. Texas requires competitive bidding above $50,000
(Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §252.021); our top tier is $18,000. Below the threshold a city manager
signs, and demo-to-signature runs 4–8 weeks. Purchases between $3k and $50k need quotes from
two HUB vendors where practicable (§252.0215), which is a form, not a season. Many charters
set lower internal thresholds — we verify per city.

**11. "89% gross margin on an LLM product. Really?"**
COGS is inference plus retrieval at roughly $75/city/month against a $9,600 ACV, and it
scales with packet volume rather than seats — which is why there are no per-seat fees. Margin
holds 79–91% across all three scenarios. It only breaks if real usage runs 3× the assumption,
which is a monitored input, not a hope.

**12. "What is your actual moat in three years?"**
Distribution and brand, not technology. Officials in a state are a referral network that
talks to itself at TML; being the tool a former mayor recommends compounds. Per-city ingestion
and budget-line inertia add switching cost. We do not claim a technical moat, and any deck
that claims one over retrieval in 2026 is not credible.

**13. "Retention in public sector — where's the evidence?"**
The 90–95% gross retention figure is vendor-reported and carries low confidence in our model;
we use 0.6%/month logo churn, about 93% annually. We have not found an independent source and
we say so rather than dressing it up. Churn barely moves three-year cash but dominates LTV,
so it is the number we most want a real cohort to replace.

**14. "You're a Public Benefit Corporation. Does that constrain a return?"**
It is a Delaware PBC, foreign-qualified in Texas, taxed as a C-Corp. Standard equity, standard
economics; the public-benefit purpose is a governance statement, not a cap on returns. It also
happens to be legible to the buyer, which in this category is a sales asset.

**15. "Everything a vendor holds for a city is a public record. Is that a problem?"**
Texas Public Information Act (Gov't Code ch. 552) means contracts and data handling are
disclosable. We treat it as a design constraint rather than a surprise: we hold the city's own
already-published documents, so there is little in our system that was not public to begin
with. A $1M GL + cyber certificate is a routine precondition for data access and is an
early G&A line in the model, not a later one.

strength — it is why the product is built for officials rather than staff — but it needs the
