## 30-second commercial — four versions

**A. The buyer's moment (recommended — leads the deck).**
> A city council member gets a 300-page packet on Thursday and votes on Tuesday. They have a
> full-time job and no staff. So they do what everyone does: Ctrl+F, then call the clerk.
> Strata reads the city's own published record — budgets, ordinances, minutes, contracts —
> and answers the question in plain language, showing the page the answer came from. The
> official still decides. They just stop deciding blind. It's live in Schertz, Texas, where
> our senior advisor was mayor for two terms.

**B. The counter-position.**
> Every govtech vendor shipped AI this year. All of it was built for the staff who prepare the
> meeting. Nobody built it for the people who have to vote at it — part-time officials and
> volunteer board members with no staff, no research budget, and a 300-page packet. That's
> Strata. Same public record everyone already publishes, made usable at meeting-prep speed,
> every answer cited to a page.

**C. The trust mechanic.**
> Cities spent 2026 writing AI-use policies, because an answer nobody can check is a
> liability in a public meeting. Strata is built the other way around: every answer comes with
> the page it came from, so the official verifies before they vote. The judgment stays exactly
> where the law puts it. That's why a council can adopt it the same month it adopts its AI policy.

**D. The capital-efficiency version (for the investor who asks "why so little?").**
> Strata is live, sells for $3,600 to $18,000 a year — deliberately under the $50,000 Texas
> bid threshold, so one city manager can sign it — and runs at an 89% gross margin. Our model
> says the business is operating-income positive in month 19 on founder effort alone. The only
> thing standing between here and there is about $40,000 of working capital and five pilot cities.

**Read-aloud test:** the sequence stands on its own as an argument — problem (2–4), the
insight (5–6), the product (7–8), the differentiator (9–10), the buying motion (11), the
distribution asset (12–13), honesty (14), the field (15–16), the economics (17–19), the ask
(20). No headline requires the body copy underneath it to make sense. Lines 14 and 20 are the
two that make investors lean in, and both work only because they are true.

## Body copy discipline
- Maximum three lines of body per slide; most slides get one supporting sentence and a source.
- Every number carries its unit and its frame (rate, month, or trend) — never a bare absolute.
- Every fact carries a source line in 9pt at the foot of the slide.
- The words *revolutionary*, *game-changing*, *disrupt*, *unprecedented* and *seamless* do not
  appear in this deck.
- Placeholders are visible and monospaced, so nobody presents them by accident.
